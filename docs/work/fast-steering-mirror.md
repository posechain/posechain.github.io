# Fast Steering Mirror (FSM)

## Summary

The Fast Steering Mirror (FSM) provides the **high-bandwidth precision steering layer** of the optical tracking system.

By the time control reaches the FSM, the Main Steering Mirror has already performed large-angle pointing, the camera has acquired the target, and the perception system has converted visual observation into an image-plane tracking error. What remains are the smaller, faster disturbances that prevent precision stabilization.

The role of the FSM is not to reacquire the target or perform large-angle steering. Its purpose is to continuously suppress residual image motion and stabilize the target within the camera frame. This is where the dual-mirror architecture delivers its primary advantage:

**coarse steering maintains visibility**  
**fine steering maintains precision**

## Why It Exists

The Main Steering Mirror solves the large-angle pointing problem. It does not solve the fast disturbance problem.

Real systems experience disturbances from many sources including platform vibration, structural dynamics, actuator jitter, thermal motion, pointing estimation error, and spacecraft attitude disturbances. Many of these effects occur at frequencies too high for coarse steering mechanisms to reject effectively. Even when the target remains inside the camera field of view, small residual motion can significantly degrade tracking quality, measurement stability, and downstream pointing performance.

Attempting to force a single steering mechanism to solve both coarse acquisition and high-bandwidth stabilization generally produces poor performance in both. Large-angle steering mechanisms naturally favor robustness and motion authority, while fine stabilization mechanisms favor low inertia and rapid response. The architecture intentionally separates these problems into independent steering layers.

## How It Works

The FSM receives image-plane tracking error generated by the visual feedback system. Rather than commanding large-angle repositioning, it applies rapid fine steering corrections to minimize image-space error and keep the target stable near the desired reference point.

Conceptually, the control process becomes:

**target drifts from desired image location**  
→ **image-plane tracking error increases**  
→ **FSM applies corrective optical steering**  
→ **target returns toward center**

Unlike the Main Steering Mirror, the FSM operates over a relatively small steering range but at substantially higher bandwidth. This allows the system to reject disturbances before they become visually significant. In practice, the architecture behaves like two nested steering layers:

| Layer | Purpose |
|--------|---------|
| Main Steering Mirror | Keep target inside observable region |
| Fast Steering Mirror | Suppress residual image motion |

This separation of responsibilities is one of the defining architectural choices of the system.

## Engineering Challenge

The challenge facing the FSM is deceptively difficult:

**remove disturbances quickly without destabilizing the system**

High-bandwidth steering systems face competing demands. The mirror must react quickly enough to suppress image motion before tracking performance degrades, yet remain stable in the presence of latency, noise, and actuator limitations.

An overly aggressive controller risks:

- oscillation
- overshoot
- amplification of image noise
- unstable closed-loop behavior

A controller that is too conservative allows disturbances to remain visible in the image and reduces stabilization effectiveness.

Because the FSM operates with limited steering authority, it also depends on successful coordination with the Main Steering Mirror. The coarse steering system must keep the target sufficiently close to the image center for the FSM to remain within its operating range.

## Why Use Two Steering Layers?

At first glance, a dual-mirror architecture may appear unnecessarily complex. In practice, it is often the cleanest engineering solution.

Large-angle steering favors:

- wide motion range
- robustness
- acquisition support

Fine stabilization favors:

- low inertia
- high bandwidth
- precision motion

Trying to optimize both goals in a single mechanism usually results in a compromise that performs neither task particularly well.

Bandwidth separation also improves controller stability. The coarse system handles slow, large corrections while the FSM handles fast, small corrections. This reduces actuator conflict and makes disturbance rejection practical in environments where spacecraft attitude control alone cannot maintain sufficient optical stability.

## Key Tradeoffs

### Bandwidth vs Noise Sensitivity

Higher bandwidth improves disturbance rejection performance.

However, aggressive control can amplify:

- sensor noise
- latency effects
- image jitter
- unstable dynamics

The optimal operating point balances responsiveness with robustness.

### Authority vs Precision

A larger steering range generally increases inertia and reduces responsiveness.

The FSM is intentionally optimized for:

**precision over range**

This makes coordination with the Main Steering Mirror essential.

### Latency vs Performance

High-bandwidth stabilization is extremely sensitive to delay.

Latency may originate from:

- camera frame timing
- image processing
- perception filtering
- communication paths
- actuator response

Even modest delays can significantly reduce disturbance rejection effectiveness.

## Implementation Considerations

The FSM controller must balance disturbance rejection, stability margins, measurement noise tolerance, and actuator limits. In practice, tuning becomes highly dependent on the disturbance environment and optical system dynamics.

Robust systems also account for saturation and handoff behavior. When disturbances exceed FSM authority, the coarse steering system must recenter the operating point. Reliable implementations treat this as a cooperative interaction rather than two independent control loops competing for authority.

## Key Takeaways

- The FSM provides **high-bandwidth residual stabilization**.
- It minimizes **image-plane tracking error** rather than performing large-angle steering.
- The architecture separates **coarse acquisition** from **precision stabilization**.
- High-performance operation depends heavily on **latency, tuning, and bandwidth management**.
- The dual-mirror architecture enables both **robust target acquisition** and **precision optical tracking**.
